How much protein do you need? A guide for Australians

By the Prepza team · 18 November 2026

The short answer

Most Australian adults need at least 0.75 g of protein per kilo of body weight a day (women) or 0.84 g (men). That's about 46 g and 64 g a day, the official minimums in the NHMRC Nutrient Reference Values.

If you lift weights, play sport or are trying to lose fat without losing muscle, research supports more: around 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg. For a 70 kg person, that's roughly 110 to 155 g a day.

The official Australian numbers

The National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) sets a Recommended Dietary Intake (RDI): the amount that covers the needs of nearly all healthy people. For adults it's:

The NHMRC also suggests that protein makes up 15 to 25% of your energy (kilojoules) to help lower the risk of chronic disease.

Why the minimum isn't the whole story

The RDI is set to prevent deficiency in healthy people. It isn't designed for building muscle or keeping it while you eat less. For those goals, more protein helps:

  • Building muscle. A large 2018 review of 49 trials found protein boosted muscle gains from resistance training, with the benefit levelling off at about 1.6 g per kg a day. Some people kept benefiting up to about 2.2 g per kg.
  • Losing weight. In a calorie deficit your body is more likely to break down muscle. Eating more protein, alongside resistance training, helps you keep it, so more of what you lose is fat. Protein also tends to keep you fuller for longer.
  • Getting older. Muscle gets harder to keep with age, which is why the Australian RDI rises after 70. An international expert group (PROT-AGE) suggests at least 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg for healthy people over 65, and more if they're active.

If you're well above a healthy weight

Grams per kilo was worked out for people with an average amount of body fat. If your BMI is over 30, your current weight can give a target that's unrealistically high, because fat needs far less protein than muscle.

A simple fix is to use the weight you'd be at a BMI of 25 instead: 25 × your height in metres × your height in metres. At 180 cm that's 25 × 1.8 × 1.8 = 81 kg, so someone who weighs 110 kg and is losing weight would aim for about 2.0 × 81, or roughly 160 g, rather than 220 g.

For example, a 65 kg woman losing half a kilo a week gets about 1,330 kcal (5,580 kJ) a day, with 130 g of protein, 116 g of carbs and 39 g of fat. You can change any of it later.

Spread it across the day

Your body uses protein best when it's spread out, rather than eaten in one big meal at dinner. A practical approach is three or four meals with about 25 to 40 g each, plus a snack if you need it. Breakfast is where most people fall short: toast and coffee has almost none.

Do you need protein powder?

No. Powders are just a convenient source of protein, and food works just as well. They can help if you struggle to reach your target, especially on a lower calorie budget. If you use one, look for third-party testing if you compete in sport.

When to check with a professional

Higher protein is considered safe for healthy adults, but talk to your GP or an Accredited Practising Dietitian first if you have kidney disease or diabetes, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are under 18.

Frequently asked questions

Is 2 g of protein per kg too much?

For healthy adults, research hasn’t found harm from intakes around 2 g per kg, and it’s common for people losing weight or training hard. If you have kidney disease, check with your doctor first.

How much protein should I eat to lose weight?

Around 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of body weight a day, alongside resistance training, helps you keep muscle while you lose fat. If your BMI is over 30, base it on the weight you’d be at a BMI of 25.

How many kilojoules are in a gram of protein?

About 17 kJ, or 4 calories. So 130 g of protein is about 2,210 kJ (520 kcal).

How much protein can my body use in one meal?

There’s no hard limit, but spreading protein across three or four meals of about 25 to 40 g each is a good, practical target for building and keeping muscle.
