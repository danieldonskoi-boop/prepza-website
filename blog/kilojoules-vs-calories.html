Kilojoules vs calories: how to read an Australian nutrition label

By the Prepza team · 19 November 2026

The short answer

Kilojoules (kJ) and calories measure the same thing: the energy in food. One Calorie (kcal) is 4.184 kJ, so to turn kilojoules into Calories, divide by about 4.2. 1,000 kJ is about 240 Cal, and 8,700 kJ is about 2,080 Cal.

Australian labels must show energy in kilojoules, and may show Calories as well. On the nutrition information panel, use the per 100 g column to compare products and the per serve column, adjusted to how much you actually eat, to count what you’re eating. The % daily intake figures are based on an average adult diet of 8,700 kJ, not on your own needs.

Kilojoules and calories are the same thing in different units

A kilojoule is a unit of energy, the way a kilometre is a unit of distance. A Calorie (with a capital C) is the same as a kilocalorie (kcal), the unit used on labels in the US and in many apps. They’re linked by a fixed number:

  • 1 Calorie (kcal) = 4.184 kJ
  • 1 kJ = about 0.24 Calories

For a quick estimate in your head, dividing kilojoules by 4 is close enough, though it overstates Calories by about 5%. Here are some common amounts:

KilojoulesCalories (kcal)
100 kJabout 24 Cal
500 kJabout 120 Cal
1,000 kJabout 239 Cal
2,000 kJabout 478 Cal
5,000 kJabout 1,195 Cal
8,700 kJabout 2,079 Cal

Going the other way, multiply Calories by 4.184: 100 Cal is about 418 kJ, and 2,000 Cal is about 8,370 kJ.

Australia’s Food Standards Code requires energy on a nutrition label to be shown in kilojoules. A maker can add Calories too, which is why many packs show both, such as “616 kJ (147 Cal)”.

Where a label’s kilojoules come from

Most food isn’t burnt in a lab to measure its energy. Instead, the maker works out how much protein, fat, carbohydrate, fibre and alcohol it contains, and multiplies each by a set energy factor from the Food Standards Code:

NutrientKilojoules per gramCalories per gram (about)
Fat37 kJ9 Cal
Alcohol29 kJ7 Cal
Protein17 kJ4 Cal
Carbohydrate17 kJ4 Cal
Dietary fibre8 kJ2 Cal

That’s why fatty foods are so energy-dense: a gram of fat carries more than twice the kilojoules of a gram of protein or carbohydrate. It also means you can check a label yourself. A food with 8 g of protein, 15 g of fat, 60 g of carbohydrate and 6 g of fibre per 100 g works out to:

(8 × 17) + (15 × 37) + (60 × 17) + (6 × 8) = 1,759 kJ per 100 g, or about 420 Cal.

Keep in mind that these are averages. Natural foods vary from batch to batch, and how much energy your body actually absorbs depends on things like cooking, processing and your own digestion. Treat label numbers as good estimates, not exact measurements.

  1. Servings per package and serving size. The maker chooses the serving size, and it may not match how much you eat. Two bars is two serves.
  2. Per serve. What’s in one of the maker’s serves. Useful for counting what you eat, once you’ve adjusted it to your real portion.
  3. % daily intake. How much of an average adult’s daily amount one serve provides. This column is optional, so not every pack has it.
  4. Per 100 g (or per 100 ml for drinks). Every panel must include this column, and it’s the fairest way to compare one product with another.

Per serve or per 100 g: which should you use?

Use each column for a different job:

  • Comparing two products: per 100 g. Serving sizes differ between brands, so one cereal’s “serve” might be 30 g and another’s 45 g. Per 100 g puts them on the same footing.
  • Counting what you ate: your own portion. Weigh or estimate your portion, then scale from the per 100 g column. If you ate 60 g of a food with 1,760 kJ per 100 g, that’s 1,760 × 0.6 = about 1,056 kJ (252 Cal).

Drinks catch people out here. A drink’s per 100 ml figure can look small, but a 600 ml bottle holds six times that.

What % daily intake means (and doesn’t)

The % daily intake column compares one serve with fixed reference amounts set in the Food Standards Code for an average adult:

NutrientReference amount per day
Energy8,700 kJ
Protein50 g
Fat70 g
Saturated fat24 g
Carbohydrate310 g
Sugars90 g
Dietary fibre30 g
Sodium2,300 mg

These are labelling references, not personal targets. Your own energy needs depend on your age, sex, size, body composition and how active you are, so they could be well above or below 8,700 kJ. The protein reference of 50 g is also lower than many people aim for, especially if you train or are losing weight. Our guide on how much protein you need explains how to work out your own number.

Where % daily intake is handy is as a quick sense of scale: a snack that gives you 20% of a day’s sodium in one serve is a lot, whatever your exact needs.

Quick tips for reading labels

  • Check the serving size first. Everything in the per serve column depends on it.
  • Compare like with like, per 100 g. Line up two yoghurts or two breads on the same column.
  • Look at what the kilojoules are made of. Two foods with the same energy can be very different: one mostly protein and fibre, the other mostly sugar and fat.
  • Don’t forget drinks. Juices, soft drinks, coffees and alcohol can add a lot of kilojoules without filling you up.
  • Eating out? Check for kilojoules on the menu or the food display tags.

When to check with a professional

Counting kilojoules suits some people and not others. If you have diabetes or another health condition, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are under 18, talk to your GP or an Accredited Practising Dietitian before changing how you eat. If tracking food or numbers is making you anxious, or you’re worried about your relationship with food, the Butterfly Foundation helpline is on 1800 33 4673.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert kilojoules to calories?

Divide kilojoules by 4.184, or by about 4.2 for a quick estimate. For example, 1,000 kJ is about 239 Calories, and 8,700 kJ is about 2,079 Calories.

Is a Calorie the same as a kcal?

Yes. A food Calorie with a capital C is a kilocalorie (kcal), and equals 4.184 kJ. Labels and apps use the two terms interchangeably.

Should I eat 8,700 kJ a day?

Not necessarily. 8,700 kJ is the average adult diet used to calculate % daily intake on labels. Your own needs depend on your age, sex, size and activity, and could be higher or lower.

Why do Australian labels use kilojoules?

Australia’s Food Standards Code requires energy on a nutrition information panel to be shown in kilojoules. Makers may also show Calories, which is why many packs list both.

Should I use per serve or per 100 g?

Use per 100 g to compare products, because serving sizes differ between brands. To count what you ate, scale the per 100 g figure to your actual portion.
